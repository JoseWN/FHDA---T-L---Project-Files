**2.1 What Is a Business Transaction?**
- Definition: an economic event that affects the financial position of a business entity
- Internal vs. external transactions
- Monetary unit assumption: only measurable economic events are recorded
- The business entity assumption: why we exclude personal transactions
- Gig economy application: separating business from personal in sole proprietorships

**2.2 The Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity**
- Assets: resources owned or controlled by the business (cash, receivables, inventory, equipment)
- Liabilities: obligations to external parties (accounts payable, notes payable, unearned revenue)
- Equity: the residual interest of the owner(s) (contributed capital + retained earnings)
- Why the equation must always balance: the dual-entry principle
- Real-world anchor: reading a balance sheet as an equation in action (Apple, Amazon, a local café)

**2.3 Expanded Equity: Revenues, Expenses & Withdrawals**
- Revenue increases equity (earned from delivering goods/services)
- Expenses decrease equity (costs incurred to generate revenue)
- Withdrawals/dividends decrease equity (distributions to owners)
- The retained earnings bridge: how the income statement connects to the balance sheet
- Service company vs. merchandiser equity structure (preview)

**2.4 Analyzing Transactions Using the Equation**
- Step-by-step transaction analysis framework: What accounts are affected? By how much? Does the equation still balance?
- Eight to ten representative transactions covering the full range of increases/decreases to each element
- Common student errors: treating revenue as cash, confusing liability reduction with expense
- Running balance table format (spreadsheet-style) — the classic ACTG 1A exercise

**2.5 Accounts, T-Accounts & the Debit/Credit Framework**
- The account as the basic record-keeping unit
- T-account format: left side (debit) and right side (credit) — no judgment in the names
- Normal balances by account type: DEADC mnemonic (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = Debit normal; Liabilities, Equity = Credit normal)
- Illustrating debits and credits through the equation: Assets (left) = Liabilities + Equity (right)
- Common misconceptions: "debit" does not mean "decrease"

**2.6 The Chart of Accounts**
- Purpose: organizing all accounts used by a business into a numbered system
- Standard numbering conventions: 1000s = assets, 2000s = liabilities, 3000s = equity, 4000s = revenues, 5000s–7000s = expenses
- Sample chart of accounts for a service company and a merchandiser
- Technology connection: cloud accounting platforms generate chart of accounts automatically; accountants customize and maintain them

**2.7 Source Documents: The Evidence Behind Every Entry**
- Definition and purpose: the original record that a transaction occurred
- Common types: sales invoices, purchase orders, receipts, bank statements, payroll records, contracts
- Digital source documents: e-invoices, API-generated transaction data, bank feeds, OCR-scanned receipts
- Source document integrity: the audit trail requirement
- Application: matching a source document to the transaction it supports

**2.8 Ethics in Transaction Recording**
- GAAP as an ethical framework: not merely a technical requirement
- Pressure to misrecord: revenue management, deferral of expenses, overstating assets

### Chapter 2: The Accounting Equation & Transaction Analysis

#### 2.1 What Is a Business Transaction?

**Core Content:**
- An accounting transaction is an economic event that (a) involves the business entity, (b) can be measured in dollars, and (c) changes the financial position of the entity
- Distinguish from non-events: signing a contract creates no accounting entry until performance begins; hiring an employee creates no entry until work is performed
- The monetary unit assumption: if it cannot be reliably measured in dollars, it is not recorded (e.g., employee morale, brand reputation, potential lawsuit)
- The business entity assumption (revisited from Ch. 1): the business is a separate economic entity from its owner(s); personal transactions are never mixed with business records
  - *Applied example:* An Uber driver who also owns a small landscaping business must maintain strict separation between (1) personal spending, (2) Uber-related income/expenses, and (3) landscaping income/expenses — three separate entities for accounting purposes
- External transactions: between the business and an outside party (customer, supplier, lender)
- Internal transactions: within the business (depreciation, inventory adjustment, accruals) — usually introduced in Ch. 4

**Learning Checkpoint 2.1:**
- Give an example of an economic event that does NOT qualify as an accounting transaction
- Explain why a business owner's personal car payment should not appear in the business's financial records

---

#### 2.2 The Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity

**Core Content — Assets:**
- Assets are resources owned or controlled by the business that have probable future economic benefit
- Current assets: cash and cash equivalents, accounts receivable, prepaid expenses, inventory, short-term investments
- Non-current assets: property/plant/equipment (PP&E), long-term investments, intangible assets, goodwill
- Key principle: assets are recorded at historical cost (cost principle), not current market value (with exceptions covered in intermediate accounting)

**Core Content — Liabilities:**
- Liabilities are present obligations of the business to transfer resources or provide services to outside parties in the future
- Current liabilities: accounts payable, salaries payable, taxes payable, unearned revenue, current portion of long-term debt
- Long-term liabilities: notes payable (long-term), bonds payable, lease obligations — introduced here; covered in depth in Ch. 11/12 (ACTG 1B)
- Unearned revenue as a liability: when a business receives cash before delivering goods/services, it owes the customer a product or refund — the obligation is the liability

**Core Content — Equity:**
- Equity is the residual interest: Equity = Assets – Liabilities
- Corporate equity: common stock + additional paid-in capital + retained earnings – treasury stock
- Sole proprietorship equity: owner's capital account
- Partnership equity: partner capital accounts
- The balance sheet equation in visual form: assets listed on the left; liabilities and equity listed on the right — mirroring the equation

**Why It Always Balances:**
- Every transaction has two effects that maintain the equation: you cannot increase an asset without either (a) increasing a liability, (b) increasing equity, or (c) decreasing another asset — always dual-sided
- This is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping: each entry affects at least two accounts in a way that keeps the equation balanced
- Historical note: Luca Pacioli described double-entry in *Summa de Arithmetica* (1494); the logic has not changed in 530 years

**Real-World Anchor:**
- Pull three lines from Apple's most recent balance sheet: total assets, total liabilities, total equity — confirm they balance
- Discuss: why does Apple carry more liabilities than equity? Is that unusual? What does it signal?

**Learning Checkpoint 2.2:**
- A business borrows $50,000 from a bank. What happens to the equation? Which accounts are affected?
- Why is unearned revenue classified as a liability rather than revenue?

---

#### 2.3 Expanded Equity: Revenues, Expenses & Withdrawals/Dividends

**Core Content:**
- The equity section of the equation expands: Equity = Contributed Capital + Revenues – Expenses – Withdrawals (or Dividends for corporations)
- Revenue: increases equity by increasing net assets as a result of delivering goods or services; not the same as cash received
- Expenses: decrease equity by consuming assets or incurring liabilities in the process of generating revenue; not the same as cash paid
- Withdrawals/Dividends: decrease equity by distributing assets to owners; not an expense
- The income statement as an equity statement: every revenue and expense item is ultimately a change in retained earnings

**The Financial Statement Connection:**
- Net Income = Revenues – Expenses → flows into Retained Earnings → retained earnings is part of Equity on the Balance Sheet
- This connection — income statement to balance sheet — is introduced here and reinforced in Ch. 4 and Ch. 5
- Visual diagram: the articulation of the four financial statements (income statement → statement of retained earnings → balance sheet → statement of cash flows)

**Common Misconceptions:**
- Revenue ≠ Cash received (cash basis vs. accrual basis — preview of Ch. 4)
- Expenses ≠ Cash paid (same distinction)
- Owner withdrawals ≠ Business expenses (entity assumption again)

**Learning Checkpoint 2.3:**
- A consulting firm completes a project and invoices the client $8,000. No cash has been received yet. How does this affect the equation?
- A sole proprietor withdraws $2,000 from the business for personal use. Is this an expense? Explain.

#### 2.4 Analyzing Transactions Using the Equation

**Framework — Three Questions for Every Transaction:**
1. What accounts are affected?
2. By how much, and in which direction (increase or decrease)?
3. Does the equation still balance?

**Illustrative Transaction Set (8–10 transactions; service company):**

| # | Transaction | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | Owner invests $25,000 cash | Cash ↑ $25,000; Capital ↑ $25,000 |
| T2 | Purchase supplies for $800 cash | Supplies ↑ $800; Cash ↓ $800 |
| T3 | Purchase equipment for $5,000; pay $2,000 now, owe $3,000 | Equipment ↑ $5,000; Cash ↓ $2,000; Notes Payable ↑ $3,000 |
| T4 | Provide services; earn $3,500 cash | Cash ↑ $3,500; Revenue ↑ $3,500 |
| T5 | Provide services on account; bill $2,200 | Accounts Receivable ↑ $2,200; Revenue ↑ $2,200 |
| T6 | Collect $1,500 from prior credit customer | Cash ↑ $1,500; A/R ↓ $1,500 |
| T7 | Pay $1,200 rent expense | Cash ↓ $1,200; Rent Expense ↑ $1,200 (Equity ↓) |
| T8 | Pay $600 on note payable | Cash ↓ $600; Notes Payable ↓ $600 |
| T9 | Owner withdraws $500 | Cash ↓ $500; Withdrawals ↑ $500 (Equity ↓) |
| T10 | Receive $900 in advance for future services | Cash ↑ $900; Unearned Revenue ↑ $900 |

**Running Balance Summary Table:** Track A = L + E across all ten transactions in spreadsheet format.

**Learning Checkpoint 2.4:**
- After all transactions, compute: total assets, total liabilities, total equity. Does the equation balance?
- Which transactions increased equity? Decreased equity? Had no effect on equity?

#### 2.5 Accounts, T-Accounts & the Debit/Credit Framework

**Core Content:**
- An **account** is a record of increases and decreases in a specific asset, liability, equity, revenue, or expense item
- The **T-account** is a visual representation: account name on top, debit (Dr.) side on the left, credit (Cr.) side on the right
- "Debit" and "credit" are directional terms only — neither means "good" or "bad," "increase" or "decrease" without knowing the account type

**The Debit/Credit Matrix:**

| Account Type | Normal Balance | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Credit | Debit |
| Equity/Capital | Credit | Credit | Debit |
| Revenues | Credit | Credit | Debit |
| Expenses | Debit | Debit | Credit |
| Dividends/Withdrawals | Debit | Debit | Credit |

**Mnemonic — DEADC:**
- **D**ividends, **E**xpenses, **A**ssets = **D**ebit normal balance
- **L**iabilities, **E**quity, **R**evenues = **C**redit normal balance

**Connecting to the Equation:**
- Assets are on the LEFT side of the equation → assets have a LEFT (debit) normal balance
- Liabilities and Equity are on the RIGHT side → right (credit) normal balances
- The logic is structural, not arbitrary

**Learning Checkpoint 2.5:**
- Draw T-accounts for the 10 transactions from Section 2.4. Post each transaction.
- Identify the normal balance for: Accounts Receivable, Unearned Revenue, Owner's Capital, Rent Expense

#### 2.6 The Chart of Accounts

**Core Content:**
- The chart of accounts (COA) is the master list of all accounts a business uses, organized by type and assigned a unique number
- Standard numbering convention: 1000s = Assets; 2000s = Liabilities; 3000s = Equity; 4000s = Revenues; 5000s–7000s = Expenses
- The COA determines what financial information the accounting system can track — design decisions made here affect every report produced
- Customization: businesses add, remove, and rename accounts to match their industry and reporting needs

**Sample Chart of Accounts (Service Company):**
- 1010 Cash | 1020 Petty Cash | 1030 Accounts Receivable | 1040 Supplies | 1050 Prepaid Insurance | 1500 Equipment | 1510 Accumulated Depreciation
- 2010 Accounts Payable | 2020 Salaries Payable | 2030 Unearned Revenue | 2100 Notes Payable
- 3010 Owner's Capital | 3020 Owner's Withdrawals | 3030 Retained Earnings
- 4010 Service Revenue | 4020 Interest Revenue
- 5010 Rent Expense | 5020 Salaries Expense | 5030 Supplies Expense | 5040 Depreciation Expense | 5050 Insurance Expense | 5060 Utilities Expense

**Technology Connection:** Every cloud accounting platform generates a default COA and allows customization. Students doing the QuickBooks demo activity will review, modify, and use a live COA.

**Learning Checkpoint 2.6:**
- Why does proper COA design matter? What happens if a business uses a single "Miscellaneous Expense" account for all expenses?
- In QuickBooks Online, find three accounts and identify their account type and number.

#### 2.7 Source Documents: The Evidence Behind Every Entry

**Core Content:**
- A **source document** is the original record that provides evidence that a transaction occurred
- Common examples: sales invoices, vendor bills, purchase orders, receiving reports, bank statements, payroll registers, signed contracts, credit card receipts
- Source document → accounting entry → financial statement: the chain of evidence must be traceable in both directions

**Digital Source Documents (2026 Reality):**
- **Bank feeds** import transactions directly from bank/credit card data; the bank statement is the source document
- **E-invoices** (PDF, EDI, XML): processed by OCR or AI to extract transaction data
- **API-generated transactions**: Shopify orders automatically create sales entries; Stripe payments automatically record cash receipts; Gusto payroll automatically records payroll expense — source is the platform's transaction log
- **Receipt scanning apps** (Expensify, Dext, Hubdoc): photograph a receipt → AI extracts vendor, amount, date, and suggests account code

**Audit Trail Requirement:**
- Every accounting entry must link back to a source document or verifiable transaction record
- In a digital environment, the "source document" may be a system-generated transaction ID, an API call log, or an e-invoice — all equally valid if verifiable

**Learning Checkpoint 2.7:**
- A business owner emails you a screenshot of a Venmo payment as evidence for a $350 expense. Is this an acceptable source document? What additional information would you need?
- List three digital sources of transaction data that create accounting entries without a traditional paper document.

#### 2.8 Ethics in Transaction Recording

**Core Content:**
- GAAP is not merely a technical standard — it is an ethical commitment to provide faithful, reliable financial information to users who depend on it for decisions
- The most common transaction-level ethical violations:
  - Recording fictitious revenues (recording a sale that hasn't occurred)
  - Delaying expense recognition (recording expenses in a future period to improve current-period results)
  - Misclassifying expenses as assets (capitalizing costs that should be expensed)
  - Omitting liabilities (not recording an obligation to inflate equity)
- All of these violate the equation — they create an imbalance that is then concealed through a second fabrication

**The Fraud Triangle Applied to Journal Entries:**
- *Opportunity:* weak internal controls; single person controlling both recording and authorization
- *Pressure:* revenue targets, loan covenants, compensation tied to reported profits
- *Rationalization:* "it's just one quarter," "I'll fix it next period," "everyone does it"

**Mini Case — Wirecard AG (Germany, 2020):**
- Wirecard, once valued at $28 billion, collapsed when auditors discovered that €1.9 billion in "cash" assets didn't exist
- The fraud began with fabricated transactions: recording non-existent revenues, creating phantom asset accounts
- Every fabricated entry violated the accounting equation — the fraud only persisted because the cover-up created additional violations that masked the original ones

**Professional Responsibility:**
- The AICPA Code of Professional Conduct requires integrity, objectivity, and compliance with applicable standards
- An accountant who knowingly records a false entry faces professional sanctions, civil liability, and criminal prosecution
- An accountant who follows instructions without exercising professional judgment is not protected by "following orders"

**Learning Checkpoint 2.8:**
- Your supervisor asks you to record $30,000 in revenue from a contract that hasn't been signed yet, to "make the quarter look better." Walk through the fraud triangle. What should you do?
- How does internal control reduce the *opportunity* component of the fraud triangle?

---

#### 2.9 Technology Context: How Transactions Are Recorded Today

**Core Content:**
- **Bank feed workflow:** business bank account → connects to cloud platform → transactions import daily → AI categorizes → accountant reviews and approves
- **AI categorization:** the platform analyzes the payee name, amount, and pattern against prior entries to suggest an account code; confidence levels vary
- **Human verification role:** reviewing AI suggestions, correcting misclassifications, approving batch postings, investigating anomalies
- **Integration with payment processors:** Stripe → revenue; PayPal → revenue/expenses; Bill.com → accounts payable — all create accounting entries from API data
- **The skills required:** understanding which account an entry should be in (COA knowledge), recognizing when an AI suggestion is wrong (equation understanding), and correcting it (journal entry ability)

**Industry Stat:** In 2026, over 80% of SMBs use cloud accounting platforms. AI automation is responsible for flagging and processing the majority of routine transactions. The accountant's time shifts to review, exception handling, and analysis.

**Learning Checkpoint 2.9:**
- If a cloud platform automatically categorizes all Amazon purchases as "Office Supplies," what problem might arise if a business also buys inventory from Amazon?
- What skills do you need — that an AI doesn't have — to review and correct automated transaction categorizations?

**Part B — Student-Facing Chapter Overview**

**Why This Chapter Matters**

In 2026, over 80% of small businesses use cloud accounting software that automatically records their transactions. So why spend an entire chapter learning to do it by hand?

Because the software is only as reliable as the humans who set it up, verify its output, and catch its mistakes. When an AI categorizes your equipment purchase as an office supply, or when a bank feed imports a personal expense into your business records, the software cannot flag the error — it does not know what the transaction *should* be. You do. Or at least, after this chapter, you will.

**What You Will Learn**

This chapter introduces the foundational logic of financial accounting: the accounting equation. You will learn that every financial transaction can be understood as a change in assets, liabilities, or equity — and that the equation A = L + E must always balance after every transaction. This constraint is not a coincidence. It is the integrity mechanism that makes financial statements trustworthy.

You will practice analyzing transactions using the equation, and you will be introduced to the debit/credit framework — the language that accounting systems (both manual and digital) use to enforce the equation's balance requirement. You will learn about accounts, the chart of accounts, and source documents — the infrastructure that makes transaction recording systematic and verifiable.

Finally, you will explore how these concepts apply in the real world: where cloud accounting platforms fit in, what AI does automatically, and what human judgment is still irreplaceable.

**How to Use This Chapter**

Each section includes:
- *Core Concepts* — the accounting principle you need to understand
- *Worked Examples* — transactions analyzed step by step
- *Learning Checkpoints* — short questions to test your understanding before moving on
- *Real-World Connections* — how the concept appears in practice and in technology
- *Ethics Moments* — brief scenarios that apply the concept to professional responsibility

Do not skip Section 2.8 (Ethics). The ability to recognize pressure to misrecord a transaction, and the courage to do the right thing, is the skill that separates a competent accountant from a trusted one.
