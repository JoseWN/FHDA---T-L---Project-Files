### Chapter 3: The Accounting Cycle — Journalizing, Posting & the Trial Balance

**Chapter Opening Hook:** *A new bookkeeper at a Bay Area startup entered all of February's vendor payments as "debit Expenses / credit Cash" — except she forgot to specify which expense account. When the CPA came to prepare financial statements, every expense was lumped into a single "Miscellaneous" line. The income statement was useless for decision-making. The cycle worked — the trial balance balanced — but the detail was gone. This chapter is about why that detail matters and how the system is designed to capture it.*

---

**3.1 The Accounting Cycle: An Overview**
- Definition: the complete sequence of accounting procedures performed in each accounting period
- The accounting period: fiscal year, calendar year, quarter, month — why the period matters for comparability
- The 9-step cycle overview: identify → analyze → journalize → post → trial balance → adjustments → adjusted trial balance → financial statements → close
- Chapters 3 through 5 of ACTG 1A cover steps 1–5 and 9; Ch. 4 covers steps 6–8
- Recurring vs. non-recurring cycle steps: closing entries (step 9) reset the cycle; adjusting entries (step 6) are new each period

**3.2 Analyzing and Recording Transactions: The Journal Entry**
- Three-question framework (from Ch. 2): Which accounts? How much? Does the equation balance?
- The journal as book of original entry: the first formal record of every transaction
- A well-formed journal entry: date, account titles (debit first, credit indented), dollar amounts, narration/description
- Compound entries: transactions affecting more than two accounts (debits or credits to multiple accounts simultaneously)
- The importance of narration: brief description of the transaction that connects the entry to its source document

**3.3 The General Journal: Format and Practice**
- Two-column general journal format: Date | Account Title and Explanation | Ref. | Debit | Credit
- The posting reference (Ref.) column: links journal entries to the ledger accounts they will post to
- Practice set: recording a complete month's transactions for a service company
- Common errors in journalizing: reversed debits and credits, wrong amounts, omitted narrations, incomplete compound entries

**3.4 The General Ledger: Posting Journal Entries**
- The ledger as the master collection of all accounts organized by account number
- The four-column ledger format: Date | Description | Debit | Credit | Balance (Dr. or Cr.)
- Posting procedure: for each journal entry, transfer amounts to the appropriate ledger accounts; update running balances; cross-reference journal page to ledger and vice versa
- The purpose of cross-referencing: creates an audit trail that allows anyone to trace a ledger balance back to the original journal entry and source document
- Running balance format: always includes current balance after each posting

**3.5 The Unadjusted Trial Balance**
- Purpose: a list of all ledger account balances at a point in time, organized by normal balance (debit vs. credit), to verify that total debits equal total credits
- What it confirms: mathematical equality of debits and credits (double-entry integrity)
- What it does NOT confirm: completeness, correctness of account assignment, GAAP compliance, or economic accuracy — only mathematical balance
- Limitations: a trial balance can balance and still be wrong (two errors that cancel each other out, or a correct debit/credit to the wrong account)
- Transition to Ch. 4: the unadjusted trial balance is the starting point for adjusting entries

**3.6 Error Detection and Correction**
- Types of errors: transposition (e.g., $324 vs. $342), slide (e.g., $324 vs. $3,240), omissions, duplications, wrong accounts
- Detecting errors: when the trial balance doesn't balance, the difference may be divisible by 9 (transposition), divisible by 2 (posted to wrong side), or equal to a single entry (omission)
- Correcting errors: journal entries with narration explaining the correction; never erase or white-out in a manual system
- Technology note: most errors in cloud platforms are account misclassification errors (right amount, wrong account) — the trial balance still balances, but the financial statements are misstated

**3.7 Special Journals (Brief Introduction)**
- High-volume businesses use specialized journals for efficiency: Sales Journal, Purchases Journal, Cash Receipts Journal, Cash Disbursements Journal
- Each special journal captures one type of recurring transaction and posts column totals to the ledger rather than individual entries
- Cloud accounting systems replace special journals with subsidiary ledgers and modules (Accounts Receivable module, Accounts Payable module) — the underlying logic is the same

**3.8 From Manual Cycle to Cloud Platform**
- Mapping the cycle to cloud accounting: bank feeds (step 1–2) → AI journalizing (step 3) → automatic posting (step 4) → real-time trial balance (step 5)
- What the software automates and what remains human: adjusting entries (Ch. 4) and financial statement analysis (Ch. 15) remain primarily human-driven
- The accountant's role in the automated cycle: review, correction, and verification
- Career context: most entry-level accounting roles involve monitoring and correcting an automated cycle, not building one from scratch

#### 3.1 The Accounting Cycle: An Overview

**Core Content:**
- The accounting cycle is the complete sequence of procedures performed during each accounting period to convert transactions into financial statements and reset the accounts for the next period
- The accounting period (or fiscal period): a period of time for which financial results are reported
  - Fiscal year: any 12-month period (need not end December 31; Apple's fiscal year ends in September, Target's ends in February)
  - Calendar year: January 1 – December 31
  - Interim periods: quarters (3 months) and months are used for internal reporting; public companies file quarterly (10-Q) and annual (10-K) reports with the SEC
- The **time period assumption**: the continuous life of a business can be divided into artificial time periods for reporting purposes; results for each period are comparable to prior periods

**The 9-Step Accounting Cycle:**
1. **Identify and analyze transactions** (using source documents)
2. **Record transactions in the journal** (journalize)
3. **Post to the general ledger** (post)
4. **Prepare the unadjusted trial balance** (check mathematical accuracy)
5. **Record and post adjusting entries** (Ch. 4)
6. **Prepare the adjusted trial balance** (Ch. 4)
7. **Prepare financial statements** (Ch. 4 and Ch. 5)
8. **Record and post closing entries** (Ch. 5)
9. **Prepare the post-closing trial balance** (Ch. 5)

**Chapter 3 covers steps 1–4; Chapter 4 covers steps 5–7; Chapter 5 covers steps 8–9.**

**Recurring nature:** The cycle repeats every accounting period. Closing entries reset it. Adjusting entries begin where the prior period's post-closing trial balance left off.

**Learning Checkpoint 3.1:**
- A company uses a fiscal year ending June 30. Describe one advantage and one disadvantage compared to a December 31 calendar year.
- Which steps of the accounting cycle are largely automated in modern cloud accounting software?

#### 3.2 Analyzing and Recording Transactions: The Journal Entry

**Core Content — The Three-Question Analysis (from Ch. 2):**
1. Which accounts are affected by this transaction?
2. By how much, and in which direction (increase or decrease)?
3. What are the debit and credit effects? Does the equation still balance?

**The Journal Entry Format:**
- Date: month, day, year of the transaction
- Account debited: listed first, flush to the left margin
- Account credited: listed second, indented (typically by 0.5–1 inch)
- Dollar amounts: debit in the left column, credit in the right column
- Narration: a brief description identifying the transaction and source document

**Example — Simple Entry:**
```
May 3    Accounts Receivable        3,500
             Service Revenue                    3,500
         Provided consulting services on account; Invoice #2047
```

**Compound Journal Entry:**
A compound entry affects more than two accounts. Total debits must still equal total credits.
```
May 7    Equipment                 8,000
         Cash                                    3,000
         Notes Payable                           5,000
         Purchased equipment; paid $3,000 cash, signed 90-day note
         for the balance; see Purchase Order #P-118
```

**The Narration's Importance:**
- Links the entry to its source document
- Enables future reconstruction of why an entry was made
- In an audit context, entries without narrations raise questions about their legitimacy
- Technology note: cloud platforms often auto-populate narrations from bank feed descriptions (e.g., "AMAZON.COM AMZN.COM/BILL WA") — these are often insufficient and should be replaced with meaningful descriptions

**Learning Checkpoint 3.2:**
- Record the following transaction in proper journal entry format: On April 12, the business pays $1,800 for three months of rent ($600/month) in advance.
- A bank feed imports a $450 transaction described as "SQUARE POINT OF SALE." What additional information do you need before recording it?

#### 3.3 The General Journal: Format and Practice

**Core Content:**
- The **general journal** is the book of original entry — the first place every transaction is formally recorded
- Format: a two-column journal with a Date column, an Account Title and Explanation column, a Posting Reference (Ref.) column, and Debit and Credit columns

**General Journal Page Format:**

| Date | Account Title & Explanation | Ref. | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apr. 1 | Cash | 1010 | 20,000 | |
| | Owner's Capital | 3010 | | 20,000 |
| | Owner invested cash to start business | | | |

**The Posting Reference Column:**
- Left blank when the entry is first recorded
- Filled in with the ledger account number when the entry is posted — confirms that posting has been completed for that line
- Creates the cross-reference that allows a ledger balance to be traced back to the journal entry that created it

**Practice Set — Month of May Transactions (Service Company):**
A complete 10–12 transaction set is journalized in proper format, building directly from the Ch. 2 transaction table. Students practice:
- Simple two-account entries
- Compound entries (purchase of asset with cash and note)
- Revenue on account
- Collection of prior receivable
- Payment of liabilities
- Expense recognition
- Receipt of advance payment (unearned revenue)

**Common Journalizing Errors:**
- Reversed debits and credits (cash credited for an inflow)
- Forgetting to include narration
- Using a vague account name ("Expense" instead of "Rent Expense") — causes misclassification
- Omitting one leg of a compound entry — breaks the equation
- Recording the correct accounts in the wrong period (cutoff error — preview of Ch. 4)

**Learning Checkpoint 3.3:**
- Journalize: On May 15, the business pays $600 for utilities used in May.
- A classmate recorded a cash receipt for services rendered as: Debit Cash / Debit Service Revenue. What error was made? How would this affect the trial balance?

#### 3.4 The General Ledger: Posting Journal Entries

**Core Content — The General Ledger:**
- The **general ledger** is the complete collection of all accounts organized by account number, each showing all increases, decreases, and the current running balance
- In a manual system: a ledger book or set of ledger cards, one per account
- In a cloud platform: the ledger is a live database, updated in real time as transactions are entered

**The Four-Column Ledger Account Format:**

| Date | Description | Debit | Credit | Balance (Dr/Cr) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apr. 1 | J1 (investment) | 20,000 | | 20,000 Dr |
| Apr. 3 | J1 (rent payment) | | 1,200 | 18,800 Dr |
| Apr. 8 | J1 (service revenue) | 3,500 | | 22,300 Dr |

**Posting Procedure — Step by Step:**
1. Locate the journal entry to be posted
2. Find the ledger account for the first account in the entry (e.g., Cash)
3. Enter the date, journal page reference, and amount on the appropriate side (debit or credit)
4. Update the running balance
5. Record the ledger account number in the journal's posting reference column (confirms posting)
6. Repeat for the second account (and any additional accounts in a compound entry)

**The Posting Reference as Audit Trail:**
- Journal page number appears in the ledger → allows someone to find the original entry
- Ledger account number appears in the journal → confirms that posting was completed
- This bidirectional reference means any balance can be traced forward to financial statements or backward to the source document

**Why Manual Posting Practice Matters:**
Even in a world where cloud platforms post automatically, students who have manually posted entries understand:
- Why ledger balances reflect all prior transactions, not just the current one
- How errors propagate from the journal to the ledger to the trial balance
- What "posting" means when a platform says "Posted" vs. "Draft" — a distinction with audit significance

**Learning Checkpoint 3.4:**
- Post the 10–12 May transactions from Section 3.3 to T-accounts (abbreviated ledger format)
- Identify the ending balance of the Cash account. Show the running calculation.

#### 3.5 The Unadjusted Trial Balance

**Core Content:**
- The **unadjusted trial balance** (UTB) is a list of all ledger accounts and their balances at a point in time, before adjusting entries
- Format: two columns — one for debit balances, one for credit balances; total debits must equal total credits
- Purpose: to verify mathematical accuracy of the ledger — that total debits posted equal total credits posted

**Unadjusted Trial Balance (Sample — May 31):**

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | $18,800 | |
| Accounts Receivable | $2,200 | |
| Supplies | $800 | |
| Prepaid Rent | $1,200 | |
| Equipment | $8,000 | |
| Accounts Payable | | $1,400 |
| Unearned Revenue | | $900 |
| Notes Payable | | $5,000 |
| Owner's Capital | | $20,000 |
| Withdrawals | $500 | |
| Service Revenue | | $5,700 |
| Utilities Expense | $600 | |
| Salaries Expense | $900 | |
| **Totals** | **$33,000** | **$33,000** |

**What the Trial Balance Confirms:**
- Every debit entry has a corresponding credit entry of equal amount (double-entry integrity)
- The mathematical foundation is solid for the next step (adjusting entries)

**What the Trial Balance Does NOT Confirm:**
- That transactions were recorded in the correct accounts
- That all transactions were recorded (completeness)
- That recorded amounts are correct (accuracy vs. source documents)
- That accrual accounting has been properly applied (this is what adjusting entries address)
- That the financial statements will be GAAP-compliant

**The "It Balances, But It's Wrong" Problem:**
Two types of errors can exist in a balanced trial balance:
1. *Compensating errors:* two equal errors in opposite directions (e.g., debit Cash $500 too much AND credit Revenue $500 too much)
2. *Wrong account errors:* correct debit/credit amounts but posted to the wrong account (e.g., rent expense posted to insurance expense)

**Learning Checkpoint 3.5:**
- Prepare an unadjusted trial balance from the 10–12 transaction ledger built in Section 3.4.
- The trial balance totals do not equal. The difference is $81. What type of error might have caused this? (Hint: is $81 divisible by 9?)

#### 3.6 Error Detection and Correction

**Core Content — Common Error Types:**

| Error Type | Description | Example | Detection Clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transposition | Two digits reversed | $342 entered as $324 | Difference divisible by 9 |
| Slide | Decimal point moved | $342 entered as $3,420 | Difference divisible by 9 |
| Omission | One side of entry not posted | Debit posted, credit forgotten | Difference equals the amount |
| Duplication | Entry posted twice | Same transaction journalized twice | Difference is 2x the amount |
| Reversal | Debit and credit switched | Cash credited for receipt instead of debited | Difference is 2x the amount |
| Wrong account | Correct side, wrong account | Rent expense posted to Insurance expense | Trial balance balances; error in analysis |

**Detection Process:**
1. Re-add the trial balance columns
2. Compute the difference between debit and credit totals
3. Apply divisibility tests (÷9 = transposition; ÷2 = reversal or duplication)
4. Search the journal and ledger for entries equal to the difference or half the difference
5. If the trial balance balances, review account classifications for logic errors

**Correcting Errors:**
- In a manual system: never erase; use a correcting journal entry with a narration explaining what was corrected and why
- Example correcting entry: "To correct J3 entry dated May 7: Rent Expense was posted as Insurance Expense in error."
- In a cloud platform: locate the original transaction, edit (if the period is not closed) or record a reversing entry (if the period is closed)

**The Closed-Period Principle:**
- Once a financial period is "closed" (financial statements have been issued), entries from that period cannot be changed without restating the financial statements
- This is an important audit and internal control concept: period-end closing procedures lock the books against retroactive modification

**Learning Checkpoint 3.6:**
- The trial balance shows total debits of $47,382 and total credits of $47,022. The difference is $360. Is $360 divisible by 9? What kind of error might this suggest? Where would you look first?
- Write a correcting journal entry for the following: On May 10, a $1,500 cash payment for salaries was accidentally recorded as a debit to Utilities Expense.

#### 3.7 Special Journals (Brief Introduction)

**Core Content:**
- Businesses with high transaction volumes use **special journals** to record large categories of recurring transactions more efficiently than a general journal allows
- The four standard special journals:
  - **Sales Journal** — credit sales only
  - **Purchases Journal** — credit purchases only
  - **Cash Receipts Journal** — all cash received
  - **Cash Disbursements Journal** — all cash paid
- **Advantage:** Rather than posting each transaction individually, column totals are posted to the ledger at month-end — dramatically reducing posting work for high-volume businesses
- The **general journal** still handles all transactions that don't fit a special journal (adjusting entries, closing entries, non-routine transactions)
- Subsidiary ledgers: used alongside special journals for detailed accounts (Accounts Receivable subsidiary, Accounts Payable subsidiary); the general ledger account shows only the total; the subsidiary shows detail by customer or vendor

**Technology Connection:**
- Cloud accounting platforms replace special journals with modules: the Accounts Receivable module performs the function of the Sales Journal and Cash Receipts Journal; the Accounts Payable module performs the function of the Purchases and Cash Disbursements Journals
- The logic is identical; the mechanism is software-based
- Understanding special journals helps students understand why A/R and A/P modules have the specific fields they do (vendor name, invoice number, due date, amount) — each maps to a special journal column

**Learning Checkpoint 3.7:**
- A business records 200 sales on account each week. Why is a Sales Journal more efficient than a general journal for this purpose?
- In QuickBooks Online, which module corresponds to the Cash Disbursements Journal?

#### 3.8 From Manual Cycle to Cloud Platform

**Core Content:**
- The accounting cycle, as experienced in cloud platforms (2026):
  - Step 1–2 (Identify/Analyze): Bank feed imports transactions; AI suggests account codes
  - Step 3 (Journalize): AI auto-creates journal entries from bank feed and AP/AR module inputs
  - Step 4 (Post): Automatic — ledger updates in real time
  - Step 5 (Trial Balance): Available on demand, updated in real time
  - Steps 6–9: Require human intervention (adjusting entries, financial statement review, closing — Ch. 4 and Ch. 5)

**The Human Checkpoint in a Cloud Environment:**
- **Review and approve** AI-categorized transactions before they affect financial statements
- **Investigate anomalies:** amounts that are unusually large, vendors that appear new, categories that don't match the business's typical expense patterns
- **Correct misclassifications:** the most common error type in automated accounting
- **Maintain the audit trail:** ensuring every approved entry has a linked source document or explanation

**Practical Implications for Entry-Level Roles:**
- Bookkeeper/accounting assistant roles increasingly involve platform supervision, not manual entry
- Skills valued: QuickBooks or Xero proficiency, account classification judgment, exception investigation, financial statement reading
- The entry-level accountant who can explain *why* the AI categorized something incorrectly — grounded in a conceptual understanding of the accounting cycle — is more valuable than one who only knows how to use the software

**Career Context:**
- Over 80% of SMBs use cloud accounting (2026); QuickBooks alone serves over 60% of U.S. small businesses
- Entry-level accounting job postings consistently list QuickBooks proficiency as a preferred or required credential
- The Intuit ProAdvisor program offers free QuickBooks Online certification — relevant for ACTG 1A students seeking their first accounting role

- Name three things a cloud accounting platform cannot determine without human judgment, and explain why.

**Part B — Student-Facing Chapter Overview**

**Why This Chapter Matters**

The accounting cycle is one of the most important concepts in all of business. Every financial statement you will ever read — Apple's income statement, the balance sheet of the company you work for, the government's financial report — was produced by some version of the process you will learn in this chapter.

In 2026, most of that process runs automatically. Cloud accounting software imports transactions, categorizes them, posts them to ledger accounts, and generates trial balances in real time, without a human touching a journal. So why learn to do it by hand?

Because you cannot verify what you don't understand. The accountants who will thrive in an automated world are not the ones who can enter data into QuickBooks — anyone can do that. They are the ones who can look at a trial balance and say, "Something's wrong here," or look at an AI-suggested journal entry and say, "That account isn't right." That ability comes from understanding the cycle, not from operating the software.

**What You Will Learn**

This chapter takes you through the first half of the accounting cycle:
- How to analyze a transaction and record it as a journal entry in proper format (the book of original entry)
- How to transfer that entry to the general ledger (posting)
- How to verify that your ledger is in balance by preparing an unadjusted trial balance
- How to detect and correct errors when they occur
- How these concepts map to the cloud accounting systems you'll use in your career

By the end of this chapter, you will have completed a full month of accounting for a service company from scratch — and you will understand exactly what QuickBooks is doing when it does the same thing in milliseconds.

**A Note on Software vs. Understanding**

You will have an opportunity to use QuickBooks Online as part of this course. When you do, you will discover that the software does most of Ch. 3 automatically. That is fine. The point of doing it manually first is to build the conceptual framework that makes you a reliable evaluator of the software's work — not to practice a skill the software has made obsolete.

**How to Use This Chapter**

Each section includes:
- *Core Concepts* — the principle and the procedure
- *Worked Examples* — journal entries, T-accounts, and trial balances shown step by step
- *Learning Checkpoints* — short questions to test your mastery before moving on
- *Tech Connections* — how the concept appears in cloud accounting platforms
- *Career Context* — what this skill looks like in an actual accounting role

**Do the practice set.** Section 3.3 through 3.5 is designed as a complete, connected practice: you journalize → post → prepare trial balance → check for errors, all using the same company and transactions. Resist the temptation to skip to the answer. The process is the learning.
